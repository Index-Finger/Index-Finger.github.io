God hates the sapper’s work. Moving mountains, shattering stone towers, and shaking the earth is heaven’s domain. The sappers trespass with picks and shovels: tools financed by hungry dukes and hateful counts. And to exercise this power, the sapper must dig through battlefields and graveyards. The sapper must commune with the demons of heat and pressure ten feet below the ground. The sapper sells his soul and becomes a demigod.  

In the early afternoon of a spring Sunday the cries of pain slowed. Men lae in cramped tents like feral porcupines, each abristle with bolts and arrows. They rocked, brushing one another with their quills and whimpering in pain. 

The Cardinal of Avignon had persuaded the Duke to pause his assaults, this being a Sunday. Bloodshed on a Sunday risks divine retribution. The Duke watched the Count’s castle from his hilltop tent. The field between him and the walls was wet with the blood of the last wave of his shieldmen. An Arrow line, like charcoal, outlined the attack’s path. The Count’s archers were prepared to print masterpieces for the rest of spring. The Duke paused the assaults, it being Sunday. He did not, however, stop his sappers. 

Ten feet underground three men peeled layers of dirt and stone from the ground. A team of twelve peasants carried wheelbarrows and cloth sacks to clear the ever piling debris. The sappers, at the head, ate and chewed. The peasants excreted the waste. As they dug the ground expanded. Earth sprung from the tunnel as they wound their way towards the Count’s castle walls. 

The two real sappers, Pieter and Engmund, were young, small, wiry, and tan despite their underground work. At camp, military men of all ranks thought they were brothers. They ate together and slept together. On cold nights, Pieter held Engmund. On these nights, just the tops of their heads and the lines of their brows felt the chill. Underneath, they were all the warmer for the cold. 

Two other teams of sappers worked in parallel tunnels. During brief pauses in their work, the sappers could make out the muffled din of the other teams’ picks and mattocks. Their direction was impossible to make out. The sounds echoed through the stone and dirt, appearing to come from every direction. 

For light, the three had a candle each. They could dig with their eyes closed but to have an emergency light was only safe. Largely they navigated by digging in the direction away from the tunnel entrance. It floated like a pinprick over an arrowshot’s distance away. 

The sappers were of higher rank than the levied dirt movers but far below the ranks of pages, squires, and knights whose blood occasionally colored the earth they dug out. Of the three, two were recently recruited to the Duke’s army. Pieter and Engmund, in better times, would have spent their Sundays designing low bridges and drainage ditches. When war came, the world ended and they were sent to work underground. 

The third was not an engineer. He was an appendage of the nobility. Michele’s father failed to pay his rents and was the last noble of the line. Michele had the honor of still fighting in the Duke’s army. The Duke gave him the indignity of fighting under the ground. He lorded over Pieter and Engmund as only the son of a failed noble could. Michele was also the only to carry a weapon. A dagger hung from his belt as he dug. Pieter and Engmund toiled under the threat of death for desertion.

Pieter buried his pick in the wall. Instead of a crisp slice he heard a wet plock. He stopped and crouched to feel the debris. “The dirt is soaked through. We must be beneath the moat. We’ve got to stop.” In the dark they could not make each other out, but Pieter and Engmund looked at one another. The bond of the journeymen was close. 

Michele crouched to feel the debris. “It is wet, but this is groundwater. Just like we hit fifty feet ago. We keep digging.” 

Pieter and Engmund moved closer together. Pieter started but was stopped by a pat on the shoulder by Engmund. “No,” Engmund said. “We must stop here. Unless we plan on draining the moat, this is where we stop.” As he spoke, Engmund scraped the ground with a hand trowel. He picked up the moist ground and mashed it between his hands. “We can start again from twenty feet back. We dig down at a grade of one foot per three ahead and we can avoid the saturated earth.”

Michele lit his candle. Suddenly able to see one another Pieter and Engmund stepped apart. Without a word, Michele looked at his two charges. They heard the soft din of shovels from the other tunnels. They felt the wind rustle as another peasant earthmover clawed a sack of earth from the cave. “Dig.” Michele pointed to the wet wall. Before the two could protest, he had blown the candle out. Pieter and Engmund began to dig again. Michele sat behind them quietly. 

As they dug ahead each of the engineers could feel the earth become heavier and wetter. The weight of the water compacted the walls of their tunnel. To be as safe as they could, the two tightened the ceiling to only three and a half feet.

At a pause, they ate bread and cheese and listened for the other sappers. Michele pressed his ear against the wall. “One of them is ahead of us, I’m sure of it. Only one team needs to reach the walls. For the amount of money I waved around to get you two in my unit, it had better be us there first. Come, listen and tell me how far ahead you think they are.” Pieter and Engmund put down their lunches and pressed their ears to the walls. Thuds of iron on rock. They could hear piercing, slicing, and sliding but it was impossible to tell the sound's direction. 

“No? Get back to work then.” Michele continued to sit and eat while Pieter and Engmund resumed their work. 

As the afternoon passed, dozens of wheel barrows worth of material were excavated. The team had dug down and, in so doing, had lost sight of their one guiding light, the tunnel entrance. Pieter and Engmund dug by feeling. Michele spent his time glued to the wall behind them, listening to the other teams. 

“They must have converged toward us. Both are so much louder now.” Michele buried his dagger into the wall and tried to listen through its pommel. The trick seemed to fail. “I am beat, am I not?” He pulled his dagger from the wall. The ceiling collapsed. Pieter and Engmund were sprayed by a wash of dirt and stone and what must have been Michele. A hundred thousand tonnes of earth fell behind them, blasting their tunnel into a memory. The wave of sloshed air pushed the two tightly together and against the wall. Pieter hugged Engmund. Engmund hugged the wall. The rumbling and falling and collapsing slowed, then stopped. Engmund hugged Pieter. 

The two knew how to navigate in the perfect dark, but that was with the aid of the tunnel's air and the unconscious connection to the outdoors. Without a tunnel, they were lost. Every foot of earth they dug would fill their hole with two feet of debris. The moat leaked from above them. It was tight. Each could feel the other’s breath. 

In the new, perfect silence, they heard what Michele had been talking about. Picks chipped at rock very close by. They listened. Through the layers of stone, they heard a great blast from one of the other tunnels. The picks in that direction stopped. Pieter and Engmund listened. They heard the picks slow, then stop, in the other direction. Then shouting. Metal clashing against metal, much more shouting. Now there was wailing, then nothing. Pieter and Engmund held each other close and waited for an end.
